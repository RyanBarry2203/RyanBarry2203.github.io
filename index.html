FEED // PALE_ROOTSAWARD
  1. 01 / 04

    Award-winning enemy AI

    Pale Roots took the EA-sponsored Best Use of AI Algorithms award at Games Fleadh 2026. It runs on an engine I wrote myself in MonoGame: game loop, camera, collision, animation, rendering and state management, with no commercial engine underneath.

  2. 02 / 04

    Armies that spread out instead of swarming

    Each unit takes at most 2 attackers, and targets are re-scanned every 500 ms instead of every frame, so armies of 15+ units split into readable duels instead of collapsing into one pile. Behaviour runs on a small state machine (Wander, Chase, Combat, Hurt) behind an IAIState interface.

  3. 03 / 04

    A boss that bends gravity

    The Black Hole boss pulls the player in on a 6-second cooldown, and answers burst damage with a repel blast once it has taken 200 damage. The fight is its own state pushed onto the game-state stack: its arena, engine and entities are built on entry and thrown away on exit.

  4. 04 / 04

    The engine underneath

    Sprites are depth-sorted by their bottom edge, so characters pass in front of and behind scenery. The camera follows, clamps to the map and hands a transform matrix straight to SpriteBatch. Combat raises C# events for damage, kills and new targets, and upgrade cards carry their effect as an Action delegate.

FEED // HYBRID_LODEDITOR
  1. 01 / 04

    The problem

    A hundred cars with rigidbodies, raycasts and Update loops loads the main thread and allocates garbage every frame. Most of those cars are too far away for the player to ever see.

  2. 02 / 04

    Hydrate near, dehydrate far

    Cars become full GameObjects inside 150 m and turn back into lightweight ECS entities past 200 m, where a Burst-compiled IJobEntity moves them along the lanes. The 50 m gap stops cars flickering between worlds on the boundary. Both worlds share integer node IDs, so a car crosses over carrying only its position, current target and remaining route, and 150 pre-warmed pooled cars cover the hand-offs.

  3. 03 / 04

    Measured, then written up

    With 100 cars in the Unity Profiler, pure GameObjects allocate garbage every frame, while pure ECS and the hybrid show no steady per-frame allocation. Editor tools mirror the road graph into the DOTS subscene, so designers edit one road network instead of two. The paper walks through all three modes.

  4. 04 / 04

    A city to break it in

    The playable build generates its own city from an editor script: a 7×7 grid of two-way streets with curved turn lanes, 912 waypoint nodes mirrored into the DOTS subscene by the same sync tool. Dispatch sends every car in both worlds to one junction; a single reverse Dijkstra search builds a route tree that every car reads from, instead of running one A* search per car.

FEED // TALLOWTHE ROOM
  1. 01 / 05

    Your life is the mana

    Your health is a candle, and every card costs wax melted straight off it, or the lives of your own cards on the board. Beat three of the Chandler's tests and then the man himself. Between duels he leaves the room, and you have three moves to search the house, bargain with the prisoners in the cellar or melt two cards into one.

  2. 02 / 05

    Heat × Flicker

    Ring the bell and your lanes strike left to right. Hitting an empty lane adds the card's power to Heat; sigils like Kindle and charms add Flicker; his candle melts by Heat × Flicker. Charms fire in slot order, so an adder before a multiplier scores differently from the other way round. Try swapping them.

  3. 03 / 05

    Reading the Chandler

    Each turn he plays one card face down and his hand gives a tell: a confident tap or a wary pull back. The tell is honest 90% of the time in the first test and only 55% once he relights in the final duel. Call it LIT or HOLLOW: right gives Flicker, wrong costs wax.

  4. 04 / 05

    Rules first, then the show

    The rules live in a plain C# assembly that can't reference Unity. A turn resolves instantly and raises EncounterEvent structs; the presenter queues them in a pre-sized queue and plays them back as animation. Turn resolution and the tween runner are covered by tests that assert zero garbage, and the 640×360 render is crushed by my own dither shader.

  5. 05 / 05

    Directed, not autopiloted

    I built Tallow with an AI coding agent as my pair programmer and ran it the way a lead runs a team: I designed the game, wrote the architecture rules it had to follow, generated content and scenes from builders, and gated every change on batch-mode test runs. The AI writes faster than I can; knowing what good looks like is what keeps it good.

PRJ_01 // Unity 6 · Team of 4 · Tech lead

Split_Shift

An open-world street racing game set in a neon city. I'm tech lead on a team of four and own the AI, traffic, police and city generation.

> SYSTEMS

  • Pursuit coordinator. A reported crime recruits the 3 nearest units within 220 m and re-routes them to the node nearest the suspect every second. Keep every unit beyond 120 m for 10 s and you escape.
  • Pure-pursuit steering. Each car aims at a lookahead point on its route and turns that into a steering angle from its wheelbase, the same geometry used for path tracking in autonomous vehicles.
  • Swappable driving strategies. Traffic, racing and police behaviour sit behind one IAIDrivingStrategy interface; police fall back to traffic driving when they have no target.
  • Arcade handling, measured. An assist layer adds grip, stability control and handbrake drifts on top of the WheelCollider car. Six handling tests drive the real car prefab and found the actual bug: wheel colliders rotated 90°, so the car pulled to one side. Cornering grip went from 0.69 g to 1.41 g.
  • Tested. 59 automated tests, including allocation tests that fail if steering or speed planning allocates any garbage.

PRJ_02 // Unity 6 · WebGL

Wrong House

A first-person paranormal heist. Rob the house, get locked in, then work out which ghost lives there and banish it before it hunts you down.

> SYSTEMS

  • Data-driven haunts. Six ghost types are ScriptableObjects, each with two of four evidence types and its own tuning (hunt threshold, speed, how often it throws things). A director picks the type and the room every run, so no two hunts play the same.
  • Eight-state ghost. Wander, Investigate, Manifest, Spook, Hunt warning, Hunt, Stunned and Banished. Hunts are gated by sanity, a cooldown and any crucifix nearby, and a line-of-sight raycast decides whether it is chasing you or searching your last known position.
  • Decoupled by design. Equipment is loaded through Addressables and reads the ghost, rooms and sanity from one runtime-anchor ScriptableObject, while event channels drive the HUD, so no system holds a reference to another.
  • Built and tested from code. Editor tools rebuild the house, furniture, lighting and UI in about 20 seconds, and automated play-mode runs play the whole heist, exorcism and escape, plus a caught run, failing on any logged error.

PRJ_03 // Unity · WebGL

Gameplay Systems & AI

A drifting car, a camera-flash weapon and enemies that react to both, in one sandbox.

> SYSTEMS

  • Arcade drift. Velocity is split into forward and sideways parts with dot products. Above 40% of top speed with hard steering, grip drops to a drift factor and a counter-force eats the sideways slide.
  • Camera flash. A raycast from the lens pulls a light "burn node" from an ObjectPool at the hit point and stuns every enemy in a 15 m sphere around it.
  • Async, cancellable fades. Burn nodes fade with Awaitable loops and cancellation tokens, then release themselves back to the pool.
  • Factories and interfaces. Projectiles come from an Addressables-backed factory that loads asynchronously, and anything that takes damage goes through IDamageable.

PRJ_04 // Unity · WebGL

Engine Architecture & Optimisation

A stress-test sandbox for finding how many enemies pooling and async loading can handle.

> SYSTEMS

  • 500 enemies at 84 FPS. Ran 500 active pooled enemies in the editor, with a live counter on screen.
  • Async pooling. Prefabs load through Addressables with async/await straight into Unity's ObjectPool, so nothing is instantiated during play.
  • Decoupled stats overlay. The overlay reads from ScriptableObjects and refreshes 5 times a second, so it costs almost nothing and never touches gameplay code.
  • Rendering. GPU instancing for dynamic objects and baked occlusion culling for static geometry.

PRJ_05 // C# · WPF · SQLite

Dying Fire

A horror visual novel and RPG running on WPF, a framework built for business apps rather than games.

> SYSTEMS

  • Chain of responsibility. Clicking an object walks a chain of interaction handlers (hide spot, locked door, pickup and so on) and the first one that can handle it does.
  • Strategy-pattern items. Consumables restore sanity and clues reveal lore, without the inventory knowing which is which.
  • An event-driven monster. A monster service roams the house on a 3-second game-loop tick and raises events; the loop turns its distance into audio cues and the jumpscare.
  • States and data. Hiding pushes a HidingState onto a state stack, and the whole world loads from SQLite asynchronously so the UI never freezes.

PRJ_06 // C# · Console

House of History

A text-based horror escape room, built to get object-oriented design right with no engine. Try a short version on the left.

> SYSTEMS

  • Polymorphic rooms. Every room extends a base Room class and overrides its own ASCII art and search menu, so the game loop treats all rooms the same.
  • Item factory. Items come from an ItemLibrary keyed by an enum, so rooms never construct items directly.
  • Sanity economy. Sanity drains every turn in the dark, more slowly with a lit candle; pills restore it, and reaching zero ends the run.
  • Enemy library. Demon, Shade, Wraith and others are built from one library with per-type stats.
